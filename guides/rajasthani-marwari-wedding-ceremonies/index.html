Rajasthani Marwari wedding ceremonies, in order

A Rajasthani Marwari wedding usually moves through 18 ceremonies: Tika / Sagai, Byah Haath, Gangaur Puja & Naandi Ganesh Puja, Bhaat Nyotana, Mahira Dastoor, Pithi Dastoor / Haldi, Mehndi, Sangeet / Mehfil, Tel Baan, Nikasi & Baraat, Toran, Kanyadaan & Pheras, Sindoor Daan, Bidaai, Griha Pravesh / Bahu Agaman, Mooh Dikhai, Aana / Pag Phera and Reception. Below is each one in order, when it usually happens and what it means. Customs vary between families and regions, so treat this as the common sequence, not a rule.

Tika / Sagai टीका / सगाई

The engagement ceremony with tika on groom's forehead

Tika (also called Sagai) is the formal engagement ceremony in Rajasthani Marwari culture. The groom's family visits the bride's home, where the bride's father applies a tika (tilak) of kumkum and rice on the groom's forehead, symbolizing the family's acceptance and blessing of the union.

Gifts are exchanged between both families — the bride's side presents the groom with an outfit, shagun (monetary blessing), and elaborately decorated thaals filled with sweets, fruits, and dry fruits. The groom's family brings an outfit, jewellery, and sweets for the bride. This exchange formalizes the wedding commitment between the two families.

Why it matters

The application of tika on the groom's forehead by the bride's father is a sacred act of acceptance. The tilak of kumkum and rice invokes blessings of prosperity and fertility. The exchange of gifts and shagun represents the mutual respect and commitment between both families, sealing the marriage alliance in the Marwari tradition.

Byah Haath ब्याह हाथ

1 Week Before

Women gather for auspicious songs and Mangodi preparation

Byah Haath begins five to twenty-one days before the wedding, when women of the family gather to sing Mangal Geet (auspicious wedding songs) and prepare traditional wedding foods. The highlight is the communal preparation of Mangodi — dried gram flour dumplings that are a staple of Rajasthani wedding cuisine.

Married women (suhagins) bless the bride or groom during this gathering. The atmosphere is festive and intimate, filled with folk songs passed down through generations. It marks the beginning of the wedding festivities in the household.

Why it matters

Byah Haath represents the communal spirit of Marwari weddings. The singing of Mangal Geet invokes divine blessings through song, a deeply rooted tradition in Rajasthani culture. The preparation of Mangodi symbolizes the family's readiness to welcome guests with traditional hospitality. The blessings of suhagins (married women) are considered highly auspicious for the couple's future.

Gangaur Puja & Naandi Ganesh Puja गणगौर पूजा

1 Week Before

Worship of Goddess Gauri and Lord Ganesh for blessings

This dual ceremony invokes two deities central to Marwari wedding tradition. Gangaur Puja is the worship of Goddess Gauri (Parvati), seeking her blessings for marital bliss and a long, happy married life. It is a ritual deeply tied to Rajasthani identity and culture.

Naandi Ganesh Puja invokes Lord Ganesh, the remover of obstacles, to ensure the wedding proceeds smoothly. A unique feature is the selection of a small boy (called Bindayak or Vinayak) who serves as an embodiment of Lord Ganesh and accompanies the bride or groom through various ceremonies. The boy is dressed in special attire and honoured with gifts.

Why it matters

Gangaur Puja holds a special place in Rajasthani culture — Goddess Gauri is considered the ideal of marital devotion and love. Invoking her blessings is believed to ensure harmony between the couple. The Naandi Ganesh Puja ensures an obstacle-free wedding. The Bindayak boy tradition reflects the Marwari belief in the living presence of divine energy among family.

Bhaat Nyotana भात न्योतना

2-3 Days Before

Formal invitation to the maternal uncle and aunt

Bhaat Nyotana is the ceremonial invitation of the maternal uncle (mama) and aunt (mami) to the wedding. The bride or groom's mother visits her parents' home dressed in new attire, with mehndi applied on her hands. She carries gifts and monetary envelopes (neg) as a gesture of respect and invitation.

This visit formally honours the maternal side of the family and secures their participation in the wedding festivities. Sweets are distributed to mark the occasion, and the maternal uncle traditionally accepts the invitation with a pledge to bring elaborate gifts (which are later presented during the Mahira Dastoor ceremony).

Why it matters

In Marwari culture, the maternal uncle holds a position of great honour and responsibility. Bhaat Nyotana acknowledges this special bond and formally invites the mama to fulfil his traditional role. The ceremony reinforces family ties across generations and ensures the maternal family's blessings and participation in the wedding.

Mahira Dastoor महिरा दस्तूर

2-3 Days Before

Maternal uncle's elaborate gifts ceremony

Mahira Dastoor is the grand gift-giving ceremony where the maternal uncle (mama) presents elaborate gifts to the bride. This is one of the most anticipated events in a Marwari wedding, as the mama traditionally brings jewellery, outfits, household items, and shagun (monetary blessings).

The gifts are presented on beautifully decorated thaals and displayed for the family to admire. The scale and generosity of the Mahira Dastoor reflects the maternal family's love and support for the bride as she begins her new life. It is both a celebration and a statement of the enduring bond between a woman and her maternal family.

Why it matters

The maternal uncle's role in Marwari weddings is sacred and time-honoured. Mahira Dastoor symbolizes the mama's commitment to his niece's well-being and happiness. The elaborate gifts represent the maternal family's investment in the bride's future. This ceremony reinforces the Marwari value that a daughter is a treasure shared between two families.

Pithi Dastoor / Haldi पीठी दस्तूर

2-3 Days Before

Turmeric paste ceremony and start of bride's seclusion

Pithi Dastoor is the Marwari haldi ceremony. A paste made from turmeric, fresh milk curd, and mustard oil is lovingly applied to the bride's and groom's face, hands, and feet by female family members. The ceremony takes place separately at both homes.

After the Pithi Dastoor, the bride enters ghar band — a period of seclusion where she remains at home until the wedding day. This seclusion protects the bride and allows her to rest, reflect, and prepare for her new life. The ceremony is accompanied by singing, dholak beats, and the creation of rangoli patterns.

Why it matters

Turmeric is considered sacred in Rajasthani tradition — it purifies, beautifies, and protects. The application of pithi by married women transfers their blessings to the bride and groom. The ghar band (seclusion) tradition protects the bride from the evil eye and ensures she enters her wedding day refreshed and radiant.

Mehndi मेहंदी

2-3 Days Before

Intricate henna application on bride's hands and feet

The Mehndi ceremony features the application of intricate henna designs on the bride's hands and feet, a process that takes three to four hours for the elaborate Rajasthani patterns. The groom also receives mehndi on his palms.

The bride is seated in a specially decorated area while the professional mehndi artist works. Family and friends gather around, singing folk songs accompanied by dholak, sharing snacks, and celebrating. The mehndi designs often incorporate the groom's name hidden within the patterns — a playful tradition where the groom must find it on the wedding night.

Why it matters

Mehndi holds deep significance in Rajasthani culture. The darkness of the mehndi colour is believed to indicate the depth of love between the couple. The intricate patterns represent joy, beauty, and spiritual awakening. The lemon-sugar solution applied overnight intensifies the colour, symbolizing patience rewarded with lasting beauty.

Sangeet / Mehfil संगीत / महफ़िल

2-3 Days Before

Gender-separated music and dance celebrations

The Sangeet (also called Mehfil) in Marwari tradition is uniquely gender-separated. The Ladies Mehfil features the bride seated on a special decorated seat, surrounded by women of the family who sing traditional folk songs, perform the iconic Ghoomar dance, and entertain with skits and performances. The groom is the only man permitted at the ladies' event.

The Gents Mehfil is held separately, where the groom celebrates with his groomsmen and male relatives, enjoying Rajasthani folk music, jokes, and dancing. Both gatherings feature traditional Rajasthani music and are celebrations of the upcoming union.

Why it matters

The gender-separated Mehfil reflects the traditional Marwari social structure while creating intimate spaces for celebration. The Ghoomar dance is a defining cultural expression of Rajasthani womanhood. The groom's special permission to attend the Ladies Mehfil symbolizes the trust placed in him as the bride's chosen partner.

Tel Baan तेल बान

Wedding Day

Ritualistic purification bath on the wedding morning

Tel Baan is the ritualistic purification bath performed on the morning of the wedding day. A paste of turmeric, fresh milk curd, and olive or mustard oil is applied to the bride and groom at their respective homes. After the application, they bathe to cleanse and purify themselves before the wedding ceremony.

This is a sacred and intimate ritual performed by close family members. After the bath, the bride and groom change into fresh new clothes, symbolizing their transformation as they prepare to begin their married life.

Why it matters

The Tel Baan purification ritual cleanses the body and spirit before the sacred marriage ceremony. The combination of turmeric, curd, and oil is believed to ward off negativity and prepare the couple to receive divine blessings. The new clothes worn after the bath represent a fresh start and the shedding of one's former life as an unmarried person.

Nikasi & Baraat निकासी और बारात

Wedding Day

Groom's grand departure and wedding procession

Nikasi marks the groom's ceremonial departure from his home. His mother performs aarti, circling the sacred flame around him, and feeds him sweets as a blessing before he leaves. The groom is dressed in a resplendent sherwani or achkan with a Bandhani safa (tie-dyed turban) adorned with a Sarpech (turban jewel), embroidered Rajasthani jootis, and a ceremonial sword.

The groom mounts a decorated ghodi (mare) and the baraat procession begins — a jubilant parade through the streets to the wedding venue. The procession features a wedding band, dhol-nagada drums, dancing baraatis (wedding party), fireworks, and sparklers. The energy is electric, with the groom at the centre of an exuberant celebration.

Why it matters

The Nikasi represents the groom's transition from son to husband, with his mother's aarti serving as her blessing and farewell. The ghodi (mare) symbolizes nobility and strength. The baraat procession is a declaration of joy to the community. The Bandhani safa is distinctly Rajasthani — the tie-dye technique is an ancient craft of the region.

Toran तोरण

Wedding Day

Groom strikes the sacred door hanging and garland exchange

Upon arriving at the wedding venue, the groom encounters the Toran — a decorative door hanging made of mango leaves, flowers, and coconut — suspended at the entrance. The groom strikes or touches the Toran with his ceremonial sword, symbolizing his arrival and readiness to take on the responsibilities of marriage.

The bride's sisters playfully guard the entrance and may demand gifts or cash before allowing the groom to pass — a lighthearted tradition that builds anticipation. Once inside, the Jaimala (also called Pehravni) takes place: the bride and groom exchange heavy flower garlands, officially welcoming each other as life partners.

Why it matters

The Toran is a sacred symbol of welcome and protection in Rajasthani culture. Striking it with a sword demonstrates the groom's courage and commitment. The sisters' playful resistance represents the bride's family's reluctance to part with their daughter. The Jaimala garland exchange is a mutual act of acceptance between bride and groom.

Kanyadaan & Pheras कन्यादान और फेरे

Wedding Day

The sacred wedding ceremony under the mandap

The Kanyadaan and Pheras are the core of the Rajasthani Marwari wedding ceremony, performed under a beautifully decorated mandap with a sacred fire (havan kund) at its centre.

The pandit begins with Ganesh Puja and Vedic mantras. Kanyadaan follows — the bride's father places his daughter's hand in the groom's, symbolizing the giving away of the bride. Gathbandhan ties the groom's shawl to the bride's veil, physically and spiritually uniting them.

The Saptapadi (seven pheras) around the sacred fire is the central ritual. Some Marwari families observe four pheras — the bride leads three and the groom leads one. Each phera represents a sacred vow covering nourishment, strength, prosperity, happiness, progeny, health, and lifelong friendship. During Lajahom, puffed rice is offered to the fire as a sacred oblation.

The ceremony concludes with showers of flower petals and blessings from the gathered family and friends.

--- Joota Chupai (जूता छुपाई) ---

When the groom removes his shoes before entering the mandap, the bride's sisters and cousins steal and hide them. After the pheras, when it is time to leave, the groom discovers his shoes are missing. The bride's sisters demand a cash ransom — spirited negotiations follow before the shoes are returned with much laughter.

Why it matters

Kanyadaan is considered one of the greatest acts of merit (daan) a father can perform. The sacred fire (Agni) serves as the divine witness to the marriage vows. Each phera is a binding promise before God and family. The Gathbandhan physically symbolizes the inseparable bond between husband and wife. The offering of puffed rice to the fire during Lajahom represents nourishment and the couple's prayer for abundance.

Joota Chupai lightens the mood and symbolizes the playful bond between the bride's sisters and the groom.

Sindoor Daan सिंदूर दान

Wedding Day

Groom applies sindoor and Aanjhala Bharai ceremony

Sindoor Daan is one of the most sacred moments of the Marwari wedding. The groom applies sindoor (vermillion) to the bride's hair parting (maang), marking her as a married woman. The couple faces the North Star, making vows of constancy and fidelity.

The groom's mother gifts a Nath or Nathni (nose ring) to the bride, welcoming her into the family. This is followed by Aanjhala Bharai — the groom's father places a platter laden with rice, moong dal, jaggery, cash, and sweets in the bride's lap (aanjhal). This gesture symbolizes the groom's family filling the bride's life with abundance and sweetness.

Why it matters

The application of sindoor is the definitive marker of marriage in Hindu tradition. Facing the North Star (Dhruv Tara) during the vows symbolizes steadfastness and eternal commitment. The Nath gifted by the mother-in-law signifies the bride's acceptance into her new family. Aanjhala Bharai is a uniquely Marwari tradition representing the family's promise to provide for and cherish the bride.

Bidaai बिदाई

Wedding Day

Emotional farewell of the bride from her parents' home

Bidaai is the emotional farewell ceremony when the bride departs from her parents' home. Before leaving, a Thali Puja is performed in the kitchen — the bride worships the hearth of her childhood home one last time.

The bride is given a dried coconut filled with coins and sugar to present to her mother-in-law upon arrival. As she walks to the car, she throws rice (akshat) backward over her head — a prayer that her parents' home continues to prosper after her departure.

Before the car drives away, a coconut is placed under the wheel and broken as the car moves forward, breaking any negativity and ensuring a smooth journey.

Why it matters

Bidaai is the most emotional ceremony in a Marwari wedding. The Thali Puja in the kitchen represents the bride's gratitude to the home that nurtured her. Throwing rice backward symbolizes the bride's wish that prosperity never leaves her parents' home. The coconut under the car wheel absorbs negativity and clears the path for the couple's new life.

Griha Pravesh / Bahu Agaman गृह प्रवेश

Days 2-4 After Wedding

Bride's grand welcome into the groom's home

Griha Pravesh (also called Bahu Agaman) is the bride's formal entry into her new home. The bhabhis (sisters-in-law) playfully guard the entrance and demand gifts before allowing the bride to enter. The bua (groom's aunt) performs aarti to welcome the couple.

The bride steps into a tray filled with milk and vermillion solution, then walks five steps into the home, leaving red footprints — symbolizing Goddess Lakshmi entering the household. She kicks over a copper kalash filled with rice and coins, scattering abundance. The bride then touches ghee, jaggery, and a money bag — each representing prosperity and sweetness.

The couple lights a lamp in the household mandir (temple) and distributes sweets to the gathered family. The groom's mother gifts the bride lac and cream bangles (chooda) in red and white, completing the welcome rituals.

Why it matters

The red footprints represent Goddess Lakshmi — the goddess of wealth and fortune — entering the home with the bride. The five steps correspond to the five elements. Kicking the kalash of rice and coins invites abundance. Touching ghee, jaggery, and money symbolizes a life of sweetness, richness, and prosperity. Lighting the temple lamp together signifies the couple's shared devotion.

Mooh Dikhai मूह दिखाई

Days 2-4 After Wedding

Unveiling of the bride and family blessings

Mooh Dikhai is the ceremony where the bride's face is formally revealed to the groom's extended family. An elder woman lifts the bride's ghoonghat (veil) as each family member comes forward to see and bless the new bride.

Each family member presents the bride with a gift — cash, jewellery, or clothes — as a gesture of welcome and blessing. The occasion is intimate and warm, with the groom's family expressing their joy at the bride joining their household. A special meal is prepared to celebrate, and a notebook is kept to record all gifts for future reference and reciprocation.

Why it matters

Mooh Dikhai represents the formal introduction of the bride to her new extended family. The lifting of the ghoonghat symbolizes the end of shyness and the beginning of the bride's open relationship with her new family. Each gift is a personal blessing — the recording of gifts reflects the Marwari value of reciprocity and maintaining family bonds.

Aana / Pag Phera आना / पग फेरा

Days 2-4 After Wedding

Newlywed couple's first visit to bride's parents

Aana (also called Pag Phera) takes place two to four days after the wedding, when the newlywed couple visits the bride's parents' home for the first time since the wedding. The bride's family prepares her favourite childhood meal as a loving welcome.

The groom is treated as a guest of supreme honour — in Marwari culture, the damad (son-in-law) is treated as royalty. Gifts and sweets are exchanged between both families, and the bride's family prepares return gifts (clothes, sweets, household items) for the groom's family. It is an emotional reunion and a celebration of the new family bond.

Why it matters

Pag Phera affirms that the bride retains her connection to her natal family after marriage. The special meal of childhood favourites is a poignant reminder of her roots. The groom's royal treatment (damad = royalty in Marwari culture) reflects the deep respect accorded to the son-in-law. The exchange of return gifts strengthens the bond between both families.

Reception स्वागत समारोह

1-4 Weeks After Wedding

Grand post-wedding celebration with Rajasthani feast

The Reception is a grand post-wedding party hosted by the groom's family. It features an elaborate Rajasthani cuisine spread including iconic dishes: Dal Baati Churma, Ker Sangri, Gatte ki Sabzi, Laal Maas, Ghevar, and Mawa Kachori.

A unique tradition is the Shank Jalebi exchange — the eldest member from the bride's side feeds Shank Jalebi (a special sweet) to the groom's family, symbolizing the sweet bond between the two families. The venue is decorated with Rajasthani motifs: Kathputli (puppet) hangings, Bandhani fabric draping, and traditional Rajasthani crafts. Rajasthani folk performances add cultural richness to the celebration.

The couple is seated on a decorated stage and greeted by all guests, with return gifts distributed as a gesture of gratitude.

Why it matters

The Reception is the public celebration of the marriage — an opportunity for the extended community to bless the couple. The Rajasthani cuisine is central to the identity of the event, with each dish carrying regional heritage. The Shank Jalebi exchange is a distinctly Marwari tradition that sweetens the new family relationship. The Kathputli and Bandhani decor celebrates Rajasthani artistic heritage.
